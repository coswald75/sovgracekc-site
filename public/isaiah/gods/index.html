The Gods of the Nations
Isaiah's Idol Atlas

Isaiah's argument is never just "these nations will fall." It's "these nations' gods are nothing, and I am God — there is no other" (45:5). Every oracle against a nation is, underneath, an oracle against an altar. This chart names what each nation actually trusted, so the polemic lands the way Isaiah meant it to.

How to weigh the evidence. Not every deity carries the same textual weight, and it matters to be honest about which is which before it goes in a manuscript:

Named The deity appears by name in Scripture itself — safe to preach as stated fact.
Attested Confirmed by extrabiblical sources — inscriptions, seals, ANE records — but not named in the biblical text. Solid background, worth a footnote.
Uncertain Inferred from regional pattern or later sources. Useful color, not a fact to hang weight on.
Before the nationsIsaiah's Theology of Idols

Chapters 40–48 step back from any single nation to make the general case. This is the engine behind every entry below — read it first.

The craftsman's god 40:18–20

"To whom then will you liken God?" — the question opens onto a workman casting an idol, overlaying it with gold, fastening silver chains to it so it won't tip.

Nailed down so it won't fall 41:6–7

The craftsmen encourage one another as they work — then nail the finished god to its base, "that it might not move." The idol needs to be stabilized by the hands that made it.

Half a log for dinner, half a log for a god 44:9–20

Isaiah's fullest and most extended satire. A man plants a tree; he burns half of it to warm himself and bake bread, and carves the other half into an idol, bows down, and prays, "Deliver me, for you are my god!" The same fire that cooked his meal is used to make the thing he now begs for rescue.

The gods as cargo 46:1–7

Bel and Nebo — Babylon's own named gods — loaded onto beasts of burden, a weary weight the animals stagger under, versus the LORD: "I have made, and I will bear; I will carry and will save." Babylon's gods are carried away; Israel's God carries His people.

Movement IThe Family Feud

Near kin, near altars. What the closest relatives actually knelt to.

NationThe God(s)Isaiah's Word
Aram / DamascusIsa 7–8; 17 NamedHadad-Rimmonstorm god2 Kgs 5:18 — Naaman asks pardon for bowing "in the house of Rimmon." Zech 12:11 preserves the compound name. Isaiah never names Rimmon directly — instead he dismisses the whole kingdom as "two smoldering stumps of firebrands" (7:4). The polemic here isn't against an idol by name; it's against a throne reduced to ash while its god does nothing.
PhilistiaIsa 14:28–32; 20 NamedDagongrain / fish godJudg 16:23; 1 Sam 5:1–5 — the ark of the covenant is set beside Dagon's statue overnight, and by morning it has fallen face-down before the LORD. Isaiah's oracle (14:29–32) doesn't invoke Dagon by name, but the tone — "do not rejoice… a worse serpent is coming" — assumes a people who already learned this lesson once, in their own temple, generations earlier.
MoabIsa 15–16; 25:10–12 NamedChemoshnational god, tied to child sacrificeNum 21:29; 1 Kgs 11:7, 33; Jer 48:46 ("the people of Chemosh are undone"). 2 Kgs 3:27 — the Moabite king sacrifices his own son on the city wall. Isaiah pictures Moab "wearied at the high place" in fruitless prayer (16:12) — almost certainly Chemosh's shrine at Bamoth. All that grief in chapters 15–16, and it never once reaches an ear.
AmmonIsa 11:14 NamedMilcomnational god, associated with Molech-worship1 Kgs 11:5, 7, 33; 2 Kgs 23:13 — Josiah defiles Milcom's high place centuries later. Isaiah's material on Ammon is too thin for a standalone entry (11:14 only) — worth pairing with Moab's week, and Milcom's association with child sacrifice sharpens the same point Chemosh does.
EdomIsa 21:11–12; 34; 63:1–6 AttestedQos (Qaus)national godNot named in the Hebrew Bible itself, but well attested in Edomite seals, inscriptions, and theophoric royal names (e.g. Qos-gabr, an Edomite king in Assyrian annals). Isaiah's Edom material never engages Qos directly — the polemic in ch. 34 and 63 is about Edom as the representative nation under judgment, not a named-idol confrontation.
Movement IIThe Powers

The empires' gods get named in the text more often than the small nations' do — because Isaiah is willing to fight at the top of the pantheon, not just the edges.

NationThe God(s)Isaiah's Word
Tyre & SidonIsa 23 AttestedBaal-Melqart & AstarteMelqart, "king of the city" — Tyre's patron1 Kgs 16:31 — Jezebel, a Sidonian princess, imports Baal worship into Israel; the OT's clearest window into Phoenician religion, even though it's about Israel's own syncretism rather than a direct oracle against Tyre. Isaiah 23 targets commercial pride, not idolatry by name — wealth itself functions as the god under indictment. Ezekiel's later parallel oracle (28:1–19) makes explicit what Isaiah leaves implicit: the king of Tyre corrupted by his own splendor.
EgyptIsa 19–20; 30–31 NamedThe Egyptian pantheonRa (sun), Osiris, Isis, Apis (the bull), Hapi (the Nile)Exod 12:12 — "on all the gods of Egypt I will execute judgments." The ten plagues read as a systematic dismantling of specific deities: the Nile (Hapi) turned to blood, the sun (Ra) darkened, cattle (Apis) struck by pestilence. Isaiah 19:1 draws directly on that memory: "the idols of Egypt will tremble at his presence." This is the strongest tie back to the Exodus material in the series' opening sermon — the same purpose, run a second time against the same country.
CushIsa 18; 20; 45:14 UncertainEgyptian-influenced cult, plus native Nubian deitiese.g. Apedemak, a lion-headed war-godNot named in Scripture. Attested in Meroitic temple inscriptions from centuries after Isaiah, under the Kingdom of Kush at Meroë. The thinnest entry on this chart — treat as background color, not preached fact. Isaiah's own interest in Cush is geographic (18:1–2, "beyond the rivers") more than religious.
AssyriaIsa 7–10; 36–37 NamedAshur & NisrochAshur, the empire's namesake state god; Nisroch, named at Sennacherib's deathIsa 37:38 (paralleling 2 Kgs 19:37) — Sennacherib is assassinated by his own sons "as he was worshiping in the house of Nisroch his god." The god is named inside Isaiah's own narrative, at the exact moment it fails to protect him. Rare among these entries: the idol-name and its failure sit in the same verse, inside the very book you're preaching.
BabylonIsa 13–14; 21; 46–48 NamedBel (Marduk) & NeboBel, chief of the pantheon; Nebo, god of wisdom and writingIsa 46:1 — "Bel bows down, Nebo stoops." Named directly inside your own preaching text, then narrated collapsing. The strongest entry on the whole chart. Isa 21:9: "all the carved images of her gods he has shattered." Isaiah doesn't just claim Babylon's gods are false — he stages their public downfall in the text itself.
Media & ElamIsa 13:17; 21:2; 41; 44:28–45:13 UncertainInshushinak (Elam) · regional cults (Media)Inshushinak, city-god of SusaAttested in Elamite inscriptions; Susa is also Esther's later setting. Median religion in Isaiah's own era predates the rise of Zoroastrianism and is poorly documented. Isaiah has no idol-polemic against these two — they appear as God's chosen instrument against Babylon, not as worshipers under indictment. Worth naming so the absence itself doesn't go unnoticed.
Movement IIIThe Ends of the Earth

The far horizon is where the evidence runs thinnest — fittingly, since these are also the peoples Isaiah says have "not heard my fame."

Arabia / Desert TribesIsa 21:13–17; 42:11; 60:6–7 AttestedAstral religionmoon-god and star worship, well documented among pre-Islamic Arabian tribesAmos 5:26 and its NT citation in Acts 7:43 ("Sikkuth your king, and Kiyyun your star-god") describe Israel's own wilderness-era lapse into this same regional astral cult. The CoastlandsIsa 11:11; 41–42; 60:9; 66:19 UncertainThe developing Greek pantheonrelevant mainly by the NT period, not Isaiah's own 8th–6th c. BCActs 14:11–13 (Lystra — the crowd calls Paul and Barnabas Zeus and Hermes) and Acts 17:16–23 (Athens — "the city was full of idols," the altar "to the unknown god") give the NT-side texture this atlas otherwise lacks for Isaiah's own day. Isaiah's interest here is missional, not confrontational — "the coastlands wait for his law" (42:4). The idol-encounter belongs to Acts, not to Isaiah's own text.

Where the polemic lands

The same purpose that dismantled Egypt's gods in the Exodus — "so that my name may be proclaimed in all the earth" (Exod 9:16) — is still running when Isaiah names Bel and Nebo centuries later. It's the same purpose again in Athens, where Paul stands among altars to gods this chart never had biblical grounds to name, and says plainly: "what therefore you worship as unknown, this I proclaim to you" (Acts 17:23).

"For you turned to God from idols to serve the living and true God."1 Thessalonians 1:9

Every entry above was, at some point, someone's whole hope. Isaiah's case was never merely that these gods are weaker than the LORD — it's that they don't exist at all, and the wood, stone, and silver stayed wood, stone, and silver the entire time. That's the mercy underneath the polemic: the invitation was never to switch to a stronger god. It was to stop praying to furniture.
