Providence Community Church · Reading Isaiah · Act IV · Finale

The Guest List

Isaiah 60 · 66:18–23 · Revelation 7:9–10; 21:22–22:5

The Bible's last two chapters are Isaiah's last two chapters, kept. John does not invent an ending — he reports Isaiah's ending as accomplished, in Isaiah's own vocabulary. And when the gates open and the procession begins, the guest list is this series' cast, name for name.

Big idea

Revelation finishes the two movements Isaiah started: Babylon falls in Isaiah's words, and the nations gather in Isaiah's words. Every study in this series was a seating chart for Revelation 7:9 — and one verse of the invitation is still being delivered.

IThe City with Open Gates
Isaiah 60:3, 11, 19–20 · Revelation 21:22–26

Lay the two texts side by side and watch John transcribe. Isaiah: "Nations shall come to your light, and kings to the brightness of your rising… your gates shall be open continually; day and night they shall not be shut" (60:3, 11). John: "The nations will walk by its light, and the kings of the earth will bring their glory into it, and its gates will never be shut by day — and there will be no night there" (Rev 21:24–25). Isaiah: "The sun shall be no more your light by day… the LORD will be your everlasting light" (60:19–20). John: "the city has no need of sun or moon… for the glory of God gives it light, and its lamp is the Lamb" (21:23).

One addition changes everything: the Lamb. The city Isaiah surveyed from a distance now has its center named — the series' last diagram in a single clause. Open gates are the architecture of the inverted hegemony: a city that fears no invasion because its King has no rivals, receiving the nations' glory without confiscating the nations' names.

IIThe Roll Call
Revelation 7:9–10 · the twelve studies, pointed home
"Behold, a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages, standing before the throne and before the Lamb."Revelation 7:9

Read that verse with this series behind you and it stops being a blur of crowd and becomes faces. Egypt is in it — the house of bondage answering to "my people." Assyria is in it — the rod, adopted as "the work of my hands." Tyre's merchants are in it, their wages long since made holy; Kedar's herdsmen, their flocks accepted on the altar; a Cushite treasurer with a name better than sons and daughters. Moab is in it twice — in the multitude, and in the King's own bloodline. The Medes and Elamites who stood first in the Pentecost roll; the coastlands that waited and were not disappointed, their seven lampstands burning on Javan's shore; Aram, whose capital road cradled the apostle to the nations; Philistia, whose beach hosted the water that prevented nothing. Even Edom — the brother, the betrayer — stands in the phrase James used for everyone: the rest of mankind.

"All tribes and peoples and languages" — plural to the end. Pentecost's policy holds at the throne: one confession, many tongues. The Table of Nations, complete, and still a table of nations.

IIIThe Other List
Revelation 18:2; 19:13–15 · Isaiah 21:9; 63:1–6 · Revelation 21:27

There is no guest list without its opposite, and Revelation completes Isaiah's judgment movement with the same fidelity. The angel's cry over the world-city is Isaiah's watchman verbatim: "Fallen, fallen is Babylon the great" (Rev 18:2 ← Isa 21:9) — the funeral liturgy Isaiah drafted, read at last over every empire that repeated her. The rider on the white horse wears "a robe dipped in blood" and "treads the winepress of the fury of the wrath of God" (Rev 19:13–15) — John naming the crimson figure the watchman saw on the Bozrah road (Isa 63:1–3).

And the gates that never shut still have a threshold: "nothing unclean will ever enter it… but only those who are written in the Lamb's book of life" (21:27). What is excluded from the city is exactly what the series watched die on every tile: the idols that were never alive, the pride that trusted them, and the covenant with death — annulled. The nations enter; their lies do not.

IVThe Healing of the Nations — and the Standing Assignment
Revelation 22:1–2 · Isaiah 66:19–23

The canon's final image of the nations is medical: the tree of life, "and the leaves of the tree were for the healing of the nations" (22:2). Not their replacement — their healing. Isaiah's own last page had already scheduled the liturgy: "From new moon to new moon, and from Sabbath to Sabbath, all flesh shall come to worship before me" (66:23) — with Gentile priests serving in the house (66:21).

But between here and there, one verse of the invitation is still in the courier's bag: survivors sent "to the coastlands far away, that have not heard my fame or seen my glory — and they shall declare my glory among the nations" (66:19). That verse has not expired. Paul died still aiming at its last name; the church holds the same itinerary. The guest list is written in the Lamb's book — and the invitations go out through us.

The series, in one paragraph

A nation is a family, under a god, on a land, embodied in a king — and every triangle on Isaiah's map failed at every corner: the kinships betrayed, the gods that were furniture, the lands overrun, the kings who knelt to each other. Into that map God spoke oracles that judged the pride, mocked the idols, wept over the peoples — and kept promising a Center. The Center came. The gods lost their portfolios; the portfolios came back sanctified; the wedding was held without a single treaty; and the city at the end has open gates, a numberless multitude in every language, and one name where the king should be. The nations were never the backdrop of the story. They were the inheritance — "Ask of me, and I will make the nations your heritage" — and the Son has asked.
