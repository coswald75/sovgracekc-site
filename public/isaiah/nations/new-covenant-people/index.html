Providence Community Church · Reading Isaiah · Act IV

A New Covenant People

1 Peter 2:9–10 · Psalm 45 · Isaiah 56 · Revelation 21:24

The series taught a triangle: a nation is a family, under a god, on a land, embodied in a king. Now the question the whole New Testament forces: what kind of nation is the church? Peter answers in the triad's own grammar — every corner kept, every corner transposed.

Exposure 9 — the map, inverted: compare Exposure 5, where the gods rode the lines inward and the king drifted. Now the lines carry worship, and the center holds.
Big idea

The church is a real nation on Isaiah's own definition — a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy possession, with a King at the center — but every leg is transposed: blood by new birth, cult without idols, inheritance without borders. And because its King needs no treaties, it is the first nation in history that gathers the others without erasing them.

IThe Triad, Transposed
1 Peter 2:9–10 · Galatians 3:7–9, 29 · 1 Peter 2:4–5
"But you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession, that you may proclaim the excellencies of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light."1 Peter 2:9

Peter is writing to scattered Gentile congregations — and he hands them the full triad, corner by corner. Chosen race (the relation leg): a real lineage, but by new birth — "born again… through the living and abiding word" (1 Pet 1:3, 23), Abraham's offspring by faith (Gal 3:7, 29). The membrane that let Rahab and Ruth in has become the door. Royal priesthood (the religion leg): a cult with sacrifices — spiritual ones — and no idol anywhere in the sanctuary, because the priesthood now includes every member and the temple is the people (1 Pet 2:5).

A people for his own possession (the region leg, transposed): the old word is segullah, the Sinai term for Israel as God's treasured holding (Ex 19:5–6 — the very passage Peter is quoting). But notice the address on the letter: "elect exiles of the dispersion" (1:1), "sojourners" (2:11) — a nation with no tile on the map, whose land-leg is "an inheritance… kept in heaven for you" (1:4), whose stones are living ones built on the tested cornerstone of Isaiah 28:16 (2:4–6) — the stone from the statecraft pages, now load-bearing. And the punchline is Hosea, applied to Gentiles: "Once you were not a people, but now you are God's people" (2:10). The triad's proudest word — people — conferred on those who had no claim to it.

IIThe Wedding That Ends Statecraft
Psalm 45:10–11 · Hebrews 1:8–9 · Revelation 19:7–9; 21:2

Act III showed statecraft's first instrument: the treaty-wedding, where two portfolios merge and the gods come in the trousseau. Psalm 45 — the royal wedding song — already contained its abolition: "Forget your people and your father's house" (45:10). Hebrews removes any doubt about whose wedding the psalm finally sings: "of the Son he says, 'Your throne, O God, is forever'" (Heb 1:8–9, quoting Ps 45:6–7).

So run the wedding on the diagram. One King — not a king per tile negotiating parity, but the Son at the one center. One bride — not six treaty-wives holding six portfolios, but a single people gathered from every tile (Rev 19:7; 21:2). And the bride enters on Ruth's terms, universalized: portfolio burned at the door, no gods in the luggage, "your God my God" scaled to the nations. There is no dynastic diplomacy in the new covenant for the simplest structural reason: the King has no rivals to marry. Where Solomon's wedding-lines carried Ashtoreth, Chemosh, and Milcom inbound, this wedding's lines carry only the bride — and she brings her dowry to him: the wealth, the camels, the ships, the wages made holy.

IIIThe Inverted Hegemony
Acts 2:5–11 · Isaiah 19:24–25; 66:18–21 · Revelation 21:24–26

Every empire on the series' map ran the same trade: keep your gods, surrender your allegiance. Rome perfected it — a pantheon of tolerated cults under one Caesar. The kingdom of Christ runs the trade precisely inverted: surrender your gods, keep your nation. Exclusive worship; cultural liberty. One Lord, one faith, one baptism — and at Pentecost, deliberately, many tongues: the Spirit does not restore a single pre-Babel language but sanctifies every native one, "each of us in his own tongue… the mighty works of God" (Acts 2:8–11). Unity of confession, plurality of tongues — Babel healed without being flattened.

Isaiah saw the retained-nations ending all along: Egypt still Egypt and Assyria still Assyria in the triple blessing, each with its own title (19:24–25); survivors sent to the coastlands and — the astonishment of 66:21 — "some of them also I will take for priests and for Levites"; and at the very end, not a monoculture but a procession: "the kings of the earth will bring their glory into it" (Rev 21:24–26 ← Isa 60:3, 11) — plural kings, plural glories, one light. The nations enter the city as nations. What dies at the gates is not Egyptian-ness or Kedar's flocks or Tarshish's shipwright craft; what dies is the idols — and Act II already showed they were never alive.

IVLiving as the Tile-less Nation
1 Peter 2:11–17 · Isaiah 2:4 · Micah 4:5

What follows for a congregation in Lenexa is what followed for Peter's congregations in Pontus and Cappadocia. A nation with no tile defends no borders — its King forbade the sword at his arrest, and its only conquest is the one Isaiah pictured as beaten swords (2:4). A nation whose relation-leg is new birth recruits by proclamation, not procreation or conquest — "that you may proclaim the excellencies" is the national industry (1 Pet 2:9). A nation whose region is kept in heaven can be loyal residents of any tile — "honor the emperor" sits two verses from "you are a holy nation" without contradiction (2:13–17), because the church asks of every land exactly what it renounces in every land: room to worship the one God, and nothing else at swordpoint.

Micah's old resolution — "all the peoples walk each in the name of its god, but we will walk in the name of the LORD our God forever" (Mic 4:5) — was one small nation's defiance. It is now the anthem of a nation drawn from all the peoples: still exclusive at the center, now boundless at the membrane.

The triangle, kept and transfigured

Isaiah's definition of a nation survives all the way to the end — family, worship, inheritance, King. What the gospel abolishes is not nationhood but the two lies that corrupted every triangle on the map: the god who wasn't there, and the treaty that relocated trust. One page remains: John's vision of the finished gathering — the nations walking in the light of the city whose lamp is the Lamb.
