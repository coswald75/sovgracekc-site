Big idea

God's purpose for Israel was never Israel alone. From the call of Abraham to the last page of Revelation, the nations are not a footnote to the covenant — they are its object. The oracles this series spends the next several weeks inside are not a detour from the gospel. They are the gospel doing what it has always done.

IThe Promise Stated
Genesis 12:1–3

Abraham is chosen, but the choice is instrumental, not terminal: "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed." Not a one-off line — repeated at every major hinge of the patriarchal narrative: after Sodom (18:18), after the Akedah (22:18), to Isaac (26:4), to Jacob (28:14). The text will not let the promise be forgotten.

The target is "all the families of the earth" — the same phrase that opens Genesis 10's Table of Nations. Abraham's family is called out of that table in order to bless the rest of it.

IIThe Purpose Demonstrated
Exodus 9, 19

The Exodus is staged for an audience beyond Israel:

"For this purpose I have raised you up… so that my name may be proclaimed in all the earth."Exodus 9:16 — cited by Paul as evidence of God's global dealings, Rom 9:17

It works, inside the book: Jethro, a Midianite priest, hears the report and confesses "now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods" (18:11). The Song of Moses has the surrounding peoples trembling at the news (15:14–16).

At Sinai the purpose is written into Israel's own charter:

"You shall be my treasured possession among all peoples, for all the earth is mine… a kingdom of priests."Exodus 19:5–6

Cosmic ownership grounds a mediatorial calling. Israel is elected for the nations, not instead of them.

IIIThe Pattern in Isaiah
Isaiah 2; 13–23; 34; 40–66

Two movements run through the whole book: judgment on the nations' pride and their gods (chs. 13–23; 34), and promise of their gathering in (chs. 2; 19; 42; 49; 56; 60; 66). This series takes up both halves, nation by nation — kinship, the gods they trusted, Isaiah's word, and how the New Testament closes the account.

2:2–4 is the series' target verse:

"All the nations shall flow to it… out of Zion shall go forth the law."Isaiah 2:2–3

Every oracle against Moab, Egypt, and Babylon that follows serves this end. None of them rival it.

IVThe Purpose Repeated
Matthew 28; Acts 1, 13

The Great Commission grounds itself the same way Sinai did:

"All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me. Go therefore and make disciples of all nations…"Matthew 28:18–19

"All authority" mirrors "all the earth is mine" — cosmic authority, then a global commission. Acts 1:8's "to the end of the earth" is the Septuagint's own clause from Isaiah 49:6, and Paul cites the verse by name as the missionaries' job description (Acts 13:47, "for so the Lord has commanded us").

Acts then walks Isaiah's map in real history — an official from Cush (Acts 8), a convert on the Damascus road (Acts 9), the coastland cities of Paul's journeys (Acts 16–19), Rome. Pentecost's nation list (2:9–11) reads like Genesis 10 reconvened in one room.

VThe Purpose Fulfilled
Revelation 18; 21–22

Both halves of Isaiah's pattern, completed. The judgment half — Revelation 18 lifts Isaiah's Babylon oracle almost word for word (cf. Isa 21:9). The gathering half:

"The nations will walk by its light, and the kings of the earth will bring their glory into it."Revelation 21:24 — drawing directly on Isaiah 60:3, 11

The last image of the whole canon: leaves "for the healing of the nations" (22:2) — the promise to Abraham, kept, at the furthest possible remove from where it started.

The whole argument, in one line

Genesis 12 promises it. Exodus 9 and 19 state it as the reason for everything that follows. Isaiah spends thirty chapters proving God means it, nation by nation. Matthew 28 and Acts repeat it as marching orders, in Isaiah's own words. Revelation shows it finished. Five books, one purpose, and this series lives entirely inside the middle of it.
