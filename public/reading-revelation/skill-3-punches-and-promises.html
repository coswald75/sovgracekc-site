Reading Revelation

Five skills, and how to practise them
Skill Three

Punches and Promises

Prophecy in the Bible is more forthtelling than foretelling — less predicting, more pronouncing. The predictive elements are largely part of God throwing punches and giving promises: issuing condemnations and comforts. Revelation calls itself prophecy five times, and a full seventh of it — chapters 2 and 3 — is direct address to living congregations.

The reading for this skill is the whole book, and by this point you are equipped for it. But not yet. This skill has two movements, and the first one does not open Revelation at all.

To those who are suffering, Revelation is a source of comfort. To those who are comfortable, Revelation is a source of curiosities.

Why the first movement comes first

That is the whole argument for the pause. Someone who arrives at this book comfortable will find curiosities in it — puzzles, charts, a hobby. The promises in Revelation are not new promises. They are the same twelve or so things God has been doing for the distressed since Genesis, run again at full volume. If you have never received them anywhere else, you will not recognise them here. So we go and learn them first, in the rest of the Bible, where they are plainer.

Movement One

Receive the comfort

Several days in the rest of the Bible, before you open Revelation

Below are twelve things God does for people who are suffering. Under each one is a list of places he does it. Between them they run from Genesis to the epistles — Abraham, Israel in the brick pits, Elijah under the broom tree, three men in a furnace, Daniel waiting twenty‑one days for an answer, a church in Thessalonica burying its dead.

Take these slowly. A play a day is a fine pace; two weeks is not too long. This is not a lookup exercise like Skill Two, and you are not trying to finish.

How to work through them

1
Read the passages under one play, in whatever order you like. Read enough around each one to know who is in trouble and why.
2
Ask what it would have been to be the person receiving that. Not what it teaches — what it was like to be handed it.
3
Then write. Not an outline of the doctrine. Write it toward yourself, or toward God: this is what he does; this is where I need it; this is where I have had it and did not notice.

The goal of this movement is not information. You are after the settled sense of a person who has been comforted — so that when the same thing comes past you in Revelation, dressed up in thunder and trumpets, something in you says I know that. That one has been done to me.

A play a day, dated, in your notebook
Play 01

He shows his presence

the comfort of nearness
The primal comfort. Not an explanation, not a rescue schedule — himself, near.
Where he does it
  • Genesis 26:24
  • Psalm 23:4
  • Psalm 46:1, 5, 7
  • Isaiah 41:10
  • Isaiah 43:2
  • Daniel 3:24–25
  • Haggai 2:4–5
  • Matthew 28:20
In Daniel 3 the comfort arrives inside the fire, not instead of it.
Play 02

He reminds them of his character

the comfort of who he is
When circumstances scream, God answers by declaring his name.
Where he does it
  • Exodus 34:6–7
  • Lamentations 3:21–23
  • Psalm 77:7–13
  • Numbers 23:19
  • Malachi 3:6
Lamentations 3 is written from the rubble of Jerusalem. Notice what he calls to mind, and when.
Play 03

He reminds them of his goodwill

the comfort of how he feels about them
Not only that God is strong and holy, but that he is for them — and warmly.
Where he does it
  • Isaiah 49:15–16
  • Isaiah 54:10
  • Jeremiah 31:3
  • Zephaniah 3:17
  • Romans 8:31–39
Play 04

He shows the numbers

the comfort of the true count
The distressed always miscount. God corrects the arithmetic.
Where he does it
  • 2 Kings 6:15–17
  • 1 Kings 19:14, 18
  • 2 Chronicles 32:7–8
  • Psalm 34:7
  • Psalm 68:17
Dothan is Elisha, not Elijah. Elijah owns two other plays — the broom tree and the seven thousand.
Play 05

He pulls back the curtain

the comfort of the glimpse behind the scenes
Suffering feels like chaos until God shows the machinery.
Where he does it
  • Job 1–2
  • Daniel 10:12–14, 20–21
  • 2 Kings 6:17
  • Psalm 73:16–17
In Daniel 10 the delay was never neglect. In Job the affliction has a leash, a limit and a context the sufferer never sees.
Play 06

He sends angels

the comfort of ministering spirits
Embodied, tender, unhurried.
Where he does it
  • 1 Kings 19:5–8
  • Daniel 10:10–19
  • Matthew 4:11
  • Luke 22:43
  • Hebrews 1:14
At the broom tree an angel touches the suicidal prophet — twice — and the ministry is bread, water and sleep.
Play 07

He speaks promises

the comfort of the “fear not” oracle
God’s habitual first word to the terrified is an imperative against fear, grounded in himself.
Where he does it
  • Genesis 15:1
  • Joshua 1:9
  • Isaiah 41:13
  • Isaiah 43:1
  • John 14:1–3, 27
Play 08

He recounts his past acts

the comfort of redemptive memory
When the future is dark, God points backward: remember what I did.
Where he does it
  • Deuteronomy 7:17–19
  • Psalm 77:11–12
  • Isaiah 51:9–11
  • 1 Samuel 17:37
  • Lamentations 3:21
Play 09

He sets a limit

the comfort of the leash and the clock
Not forever — just for now. Suffering that has been foretold is suffering that has been contained.
Where he does it
  • Genesis 15:13–16
  • Exodus 12:40–41
  • Jeremiah 29:10
  • Daniel 9:2
  • Job 1:12; 2:6
  • Psalm 30:5
  • Isaiah 54:7–8
  • Mark 13:20
  • 1 Peter 1:6
  • 1 Peter 5:10
Genesis 15 dates the affliction four hundred years before it exists, and Exodus 12 is the receipt: “at the end of 430 years, on that very day.” The comfort was given to the father so the children could hold it in the brick pits.
Play 10

He promises vindication

the comfort of justice
Judgment texts are comfort texts, addressed to the wronged.
Where he does it
  • Deuteronomy 32:35–36
  • Psalm 73:17–20
  • Isaiah 35:4
  • Luke 18:7–8
  • 2 Thessalonians 1:5–7
Isaiah 35:4 puts vengeance and salvation in one breath, both offered as comfort.
Play 11

He gives brothers in the fire

the comfort of companions
God rarely comforts in isolation; he shows the sufferer the fellowship.
Where he does it
  • 1 Kings 19:18
  • Daniel 3:16–25
  • Philippians 1:14
  • 1 Peter 5:9
Three men were thrown in together. The furnace is shared before the fourth man appears.
Play 12

He shows them the end

the comfort of the last page
God lifts the sufferer’s eyes over the wall of the present to the consummation.
Where he does it
  • Isaiah 25:6–9
  • Isaiah 65:17–19
  • Psalm 73:24
  • Romans 8:18
  • 2 Corinthians 4:16–18
  • 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18
1 Thessalonians 4 ends the fullest eschatological passage in Paul’s early letters not with “therefore make a chart” but with “therefore comfort one another with these words.”

Before you go on

Read back over what you wrote. Which of the twelve did you have the least to say about? That is usually the one you have not needed yet — or the one you have needed and never named. Either way, mark it. You will want to know which it was when you meet it in chapter 7, or 12, or 21.

Mark the thin one
Movement Two

Hunt for them in Revelation

All twenty‑two chapters, in order, over as long as it takes

Now read Revelation entirely — every chapter, in order, nothing skipped. Take your time over it. A chapter a sitting is a good pace, and there is nothing wrong with staying in one chapter for a week if it holds you. Some weeks you will read four. Some weeks you will read none. Pick up where you left off rather than starting again.

What matters is the order and the finishing, not the speed. This book pays things back across long distances — something handed to Smyrna in chapter 2 comes due in chapter 21 — and you will only feel that if you have come the whole way to get there.

You are hunting. Every time one of the twelve goes past, note the play number and the chapter and verse. That is the whole method. Some of them are declared outright. Some are buried in a phrase. Some are the entire point of a chapter you thought was about something else.

What to do

1
Keep the list of twelve on one page of your notebook, numbered, with room under each.
2
When one goes past, write the reference under its number — a reference and a few words, no more. The hunt runs light. Anything that stops you can be sat with as long as you like, but that is a separate page.
3
Do the same with the punches — the condemnations. You do not have a list for those yet, so just mark them: who is this against, and what is he charging them with?
4
When you reach chapter 22, look back at your page. Which plays are crowded? Which are thin? Where did you expect one and not find it?
One page, twelve numbers, filled in over months

There is no answer key here, and that is deliberate. An answer key would turn the hunt into a checking exercise — you would stop reading Revelation and start grading yourself against a list. What you find yourself, you keep. Bring your page to church and we will compare notes.

The punches. You will have noticed this pass runs lopsided — twelve comforts, and nothing like a list for the condemnations. That is deliberate. The one place in Revelation where the punches come labelled — named recipient, named charge, named remedy — is chapters 2 and 3, and that is where Skill Four picks them up.
